The meta-skill · 8 minutes

Asking Claude harder questions.

Cost · $0 Needed · something you've made Difficulty · easy to start, deep to master

You can have a great relationship with Claude using only simple questions. But there's a meta-skill that separates good users from great ones — knowing how to ask Claude harder, weirder, more demanding things. This chapter teaches one of the most useful versions of that skill: the board-of-directors review.

The basic move

Claude is a very good roleplayer. When you ask it to pretend to be someone with a specific perspective — and to critique your work from that perspective — you get feedback that's wildly more useful than "what do you think of this?"

The shape of the prompt is:

"Pretend you are [PERSON OR ROLE]. From that perspective, review [THE THING I'M GIVING YOU]. Be specific. Be critical. Don't soften."

That's it. That's the move.

A real example — the board of directors review

The author of this site used this exact technique to improve what you're reading. The prompt was something like:

"Conduct a full review of the current site from a virtual board of directors. Have Steve Wozniak, Steve Jobs, and Richard Feynman each give their critique. Be specific. Don't merge them — let each one have their own voice."

What came back was three independent reads of the same work, each from a different angle:

  • Woz cared about accessibility — was every new word defined, did beginners have a path?
  • Jobs cared about destination and ruthless editing — was every line earning its place?
  • Feynman cared about honesty and comprehension — could the reader actually do the thing afterward?

The result: a sharper, more honest, more useful site than any single round of critique could have produced. (Including chapters 03, 05, and 07 — which exist because the board insisted.)

Who to put on your board

The trick is picking people whose perspectives genuinely clash with each other and with your own blind spots. A few categories to mix from:

  • The skeptic. Someone who hates everything until proven otherwise. "Have a cynical newspaper editor tear this apart."
  • The novice. Someone who is exactly your target reader and is allowed to be confused. "Pretend you're my 70-year-old aunt who has never used a terminal."
  • The expert. Someone whose standards are intimidatingly high. "Pretend you're a senior engineer at a big tech company."
  • The artist. Someone who cares about taste. "Pretend you're the editor of a literary magazine."
  • The historical figure. Someone whose work you admire. "Pretend you're Steve Jobs in 2007. What's missing?"

The point isn't to channel real people accurately — it's to use the names as shortcuts for distinct mental modes.

A few more uses for this technique

  • Writing: "Pretend you're an editor at The New Yorker. Where does this lose me?"
  • Code: "Pretend you're a security engineer. Where could this break?"
  • Plans: "Pretend you're the person who has to execute this. What's unrealistic?"
  • Decisions: "Give me four different stakeholders' takes on this choice."
  • Self-reflection: "Pretend you're a kind, wise mentor. What would you say to me about how I'm spending my time?"
Try this now Pick anything you've made recently — a piece of writing, an email draft, a plan, a piece of code, a slide deck. Go to Claude (claude.ai or Claude Code) and ask: 
Review this from the perspective of three different people:
1. [a skeptic]
2. [a novice]
3. [an expert]

Let each voice be distinct. Be specific. Don't merge them.

[paste your thing here]
See what comes back.

Why this works (a Feynman note)

The reason this technique works is that Claude has read an enormous amount of writing — including writing by and about all the people you might ask it to roleplay. When you say "what would Feynman say," you're not getting the real Feynman; you're getting a distillation of how a careful, honest, curious thinker tends to evaluate work.

That distillation is genuinely useful, even when imperfect.

The closing note

This is what 1-to-100 looks like in practice: less "ask Claude for help with this thing," more "use Claude to make my thinking sharper." When you stop asking Claude for answers and start asking it for critiques, alternatives, and pressure tests, you graduate from user to collaborator.
